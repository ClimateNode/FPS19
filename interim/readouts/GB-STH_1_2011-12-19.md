### Copperfield Road, Southampton Unitary Authority
* {01 Sep 2007 - 30 Sep 2007}: A flooding incident was recorded on Copperfield Road. (p2)

### Daisy Dip
* Fluvial flooding of an ordinary watercourse triggered by heavy downpours. Large volumes of surface water were discharged into the watercourse from surrounding surface water drains and surface runoff from gardens and Daisy Dip Park. The event was exacerbated by rubbish and debris blocking the river channel, culverts, piped sections under footpaths/bridges and the trash screen. (p1) [PAG]
* {general}: The area experiences frequent flooding incidents; the ordinary watercourse and its tributaries run along the length of Daisy Dip/Bassett Wood, contributing to inundation of the natural floodplain, footpaths and bridges. (p2) [IMG]
* Daisy Dip is the natural low point where the converged watercourse flows; it forms the local floodplain and receives drainage (natural and man‑made) from surrounding built areas during heavy downpours. Conveyance is limited by culverts and piped sections so flows can accumulate at Daisy Dip and discharge into the culvert under Lobelia Road. (p3) [PAG]
* {general}: Runoff from the park into the adjacent watercourse increases water volume in the channel during and after rainfall, placing the footpath/pedestrian access routes at risk of inundation. Conveyance can be reduced by debris obstructing the channel, culverts, trash screens and pipes. Management recommendations (allowing grass to remain longer) aim to slow runoff; potential future works could include enlarging culverts and pipes under bridges/footpaths to improve conveyance. (p4)

### Daisy Dip Park
* Surface runoff from gardens and a spring within Daisy Dip Park discharged into the watercourse during heavy downpours and contributed to the fluvial flooding. Blocked culverts and piped sections under footpaths in the park area exacerbated flow and blockage issues. (p1) [PAG]
* {general}: Footpaths, small bridges and the park's natural floodplain were inundated. Spring-fed surface water runoff within the park was observed flowing toward paths; blocked piped sections beneath paths led to localised standing water and puddling, reducing accessibility within the park. (p2) [IMG]
* The park area is a natural low point that receives drainage from surrounding built areas; it forms the natural floodplain and is subject to increased surface water discharge during heavy downpours. Runoff is slowed by grassed areas, but culvert/piped conveyance limits mean the park can become inundated when flows are high or blocked. (p3)

### Bassett Wood
* {general}: Footpaths, small bridges and the natural floodplain within Bassett Wood / Daisy Dip were flooded. Surface water runoff (including runoff from gardens and a spring) is channelled into the ordinary watercourse running through the wood; blocked piped sections beneath footpaths caused localised ponding. Culverts (including one under a foot bridge) and trash screens control flow but debris/blocked pipes have contributed to local flooding. Open Spaces staff report that flooding incidents at Daisy Dip occur frequently. (p2)
* One tributary flows through Bassett Wood, which contains small culverted and piped sections; the natural meandering channel and adjacent areas act as natural floodplain during extreme flows, so surface water is directed into the watercourse and nearby areas can inundate when conveyance is restricted. (p3) [PAG]

### Monks Brook
* Monks Brook is the receiving watercourse for the culverted flow from Daisy Dip (via the Lobelia Road culvert) and thus conveys the discharged flows eastwards toward the River Itchen; it carried increased flows associated with the event. (p3)

### Southern Water
* Surface water drains (noted as the responsibility of Southern Water) discharged large quantities of runoff into the ordinary watercourse during heavy downpours, contributing to the fluvial flooding at Daisy Dip. (p1)

### Culvert
* {general}: Culverts under footbridges were conveying water from gardens and the watercourse; blocked or piped sections beneath footpaths were identified and associated with localised water accumulation and flooding beneath paths. (p2) [IMG]
